Pitch Deck Studio
Rooks Productions

The 10-Slide Method

Pitch deck architecture used by studios, financiers, and production companies. Each slide has a clear job — respect the sequence.

Slide 01
Title Page

The first impression. Title, writer name, genre, and budget tier. No clutter. The visual identity of the entire deck starts here.

Treat this like a movie poster — the title should carry weight. Use the display typeface at maximum size.
Slide 02
Logline

One to two sentences. Protagonist, goal, obstacle, stakes. A room should understand the entire film from these lines alone.

Read it aloud. If it takes more than one breath, it's too long.
Slide 03
The Story

Three acts in three paragraphs. Setup, escalation, resolution. Don't summarize every scene — hit the structural peaks and emotional turns that make financiers lean in.

End each act description on a question that only the next act can answer.
Slide 04
Protagonist

Want (external goal) vs. Need (internal wound). The flaw that creates all their problems. The transformation that makes the ending meaningful.

If you can't articulate the protagonist's wound in one sentence, the screenplay has a character problem.
Slide 05
World & Tone

The visual and emotional world of the film. Time period, setting, atmosphere. This slide should feel like the trailer — immersive, specific, unmistakable.

Tone keywords should be contradictions: "intimate epic," "funny bleak," "quiet explosive."
Slide 06
Comparable Films
×

Two to three films that bracket the tone, budget range, and audience. Not "I want my film to be as successful as…" — these are reference points for the buyer, not aspirations.

The formula: "A meets B, set in C" works only when A and B are genuinely unexpected together.
Slide 07
Why Now

Cultural timing, audience need, market gap. Why does this story resonate in this specific moment? Specificity signals research. Generality signals filler.

Cite a real cultural phenomenon, not a mood. "In an era of…" is a red flag.
Slide 08
Target Market

Primary audience, secondary audience, distribution target (theatrical / streaming / festival), MPAA rating target, budget range. This is the business slide — make it look like you've done the math.

Name the platform you're pitching toward. "Netflix-ready" tells them more than "wide release."
Slide 09
The Team

Writer bio, director (attached or seeking), producer, key talent. Leads with credits, not aspirations. If the team is emerging, lead with the story's strength and the comp titles.

One strong producing credit is worth more than five festival mentions. Lead with it.
Slide 10
Contact & Next Steps

Clean contact block: email, WGA registration, website / IMDb. State that the full screenplay is available on request under WGA submission agreement. End confidently — no hedging.

This slide should feel like the last frame of a great trailer. Leave them wanting to make the call.

Producer Query Letter

Typography

Type choices tell the reader what kind of film this is before a single word of story lands.

Display / Title
BARLOW CONDENSED

Use for film title, act labels, section headers. All caps. Maximum weight. Condensed sans gives scale without clutter — the language of the theatrical poster.

Body / Story Text
Georgia — story and description copy

Serif body text signals craft and permanence. Use Georgia for logline, story description, and any long-form copy. Set at 13–15px with 1.7 line-height minimum.

Labels / Metadata
IBM PLEX MONO — SLIDE 01 — GENRE

Monospace for all metadata, labels, tags, and functional annotation. Creates technical precision that reads as authority. Use gold (#D4A034) for key labels only.

Quote / Tip
"The title should carry weight. Use the display typeface at maximum size."

Italic Georgia for blockquotes and director's notes. Left border in muted gold. This creates hierarchy without competing with primary content.

Visual Palettes

Choose one palette per project. Consistency signals professionalism. Mixing palettes signals amateur work.

Studio Dark

Default Rooks palette. Deep navy-black backgrounds, gold accent. Used for drama, crime, prestige.

Midnight Purple

Sci-fi, horror, psychological thriller. Deep indigo with violet highlight. High-contrast feel.

Forest / Natural

Westerns, outdoor survival, coming-of-age. Earth tones with natural green. Intimate and grounded.

Amber / Neo-Noir

Crime, biopic, action. Warm amber-orange on near-black. High tension, street-level energy.

Deck Principles

Rules that separate professional decks from amateur ones. Break them consciously — but know them first.

One image per slide — maximum.

A mood board belongs in a separate document. Every image on a pitch slide must earn its place by communicating something words cannot. More than one image per slide creates chaos.

White space is not wasted space.

A financier reading a dense deck loses the thread. A clean slide makes the one idea on it land harder. Resist filling every corner — your restraint signals confidence in the material.

Every slide has one job. Name it before you design it.

Before placing a single element, write the slide's job in one sentence: "This slide makes them believe the character's wound is real." If you can't write that sentence, you don't know what the slide is for.

Comp titles are positioning, not aspiration.

Citing The Godfather tells a financier you don't understand how pitching works. Comp titles bracket a commercial range and an audience expectation — not a quality target. Use films from the last five to seven years.

The deck is not the screenplay. Don't summarize every beat.

A pitch deck creates desire to read the screenplay — it doesn't replace it. Three sentences of story are enough. If the deck tells the whole film, there's no reason to ask for the script.

End on confidence, not on hope.

The last slide is a statement of availability, not a plea. "The screenplay is available immediately for review under standard WGA agreement." Full stop. Never "I hope you'll consider…" — that sentence belongs in the cover email, not the deck.
